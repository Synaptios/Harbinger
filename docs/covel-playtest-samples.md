## Unexpected invitation

**Player input** I ask Alice, “Will you come to supper after you’re finished here?”

### Version A

“Will you come to supper after you’re finished here?” you ask Alice.

She has one hand on the rim of the tray. At the word supper she looks up, though her hand stays where it is.

“How late can you keep a bowl?” she asks. “I can’t give you an hour from the look of this.”

She lifts the cloth no higher than she needs to. You can see the two hollows her fingers left in the dough. One has all but risen out; the other holds a little shadow. She lets the cloth fall again, this time with the corner clear of the splinter.

“After I’m finished,” she says. “You make that sound like a time of day.”

There is a little breath of laughter in it. She shifts the tray away from the draught under the door, drawing it toward her by the two short ends. It takes both hands. When she has it where she wants it, she slips the flat of her palm out from underneath, careful not to jar the board.

“Would you be sitting down too?”

She asks it with her eyes on the tray. Then she looks at you and waits long enough that there is no mistaking the question for a way of asking who else might be there.

The heat from the oven reaches only one side of the board. On the other, the loaf Alice set apart has stopped giving off steam. She turns it a quarter-turn with her fingertips. A thin flake comes loose at the split in the crust. She catches it against the board and eats it, and the small sound it makes between her teeth is clear in the quiet the crier left behind.

“I don’t want you keeping the pot on for me,” she says. “That’s wood spent twice on one meal.”

She begins to sweep the crumbs together with the side of her hand. At the edge of the flour she stops. The book is well out of her way, but the mark it left before Martin moved it has a straightness none of the other marks on the board have. She runs her hand along beside it without crossing it, and tips the crumbs into her palm.

“At dusk he’ll want his sums,” she says. “I heard him.”

She rubs the crumbs between finger and thumb. The finest fall through, and she holds her palm level to keep the rest from following. She has not asked where you mean to eat, or what there would be in the bowl. You can see where the flour has settled in the creases of her wrist, just above the place her apron failed to wipe clean.

“Cold’s no trouble,” she says. “Only tell me if you mean to sit down with me.”

### Version B

“I can be done with the bread,” she says. “That doesn’t mean I’ll be done here. If he’s got a question about a sack, I want him to ask it while I’m standing by the scales. He needn’t send for me tomorrow when the sack’s empty.”

She takes the weight off the board and sets it in the lower pan. The chain gives a small rattle. With the other pan held steady in her hand she teases the scrap out of its fold. The crier had put no pause between the news of the king’s death and the name of the man he said the king had fought. Alice draws the cloth off one corner of the tray and touches the dough. “Not yet.” She covers it again. “You can ask me to supper without coming to the river. I know that much.”

She picks up the loaf with the crack in it. This time she holds it by both ends and looks down into the split before setting it back. “I’ll bring bread if there’s any left. If there isn’t, I’ll come all the same. That suit?”

## Refusal and departure

**Player input** Return to the bakehouse with the ledger

### Version A

You return to the bakehouse with Alice, the ledger tucked under your arm. The dusk bell is still sounding when the boy catches you at the mouth of the lane. He puts the key into Alice’s palm bit first. She takes it as she would take a hot thing, by the part he has left for her to hold.

“Martin ordered the oven sealed,” the boy says. “While you were away. There’s an order on the shutter. I saw it pinned there.”

The last stroke of the bell leaves him speaking louder than he needs to. Alice turns the key over. Flour has worked into the wards; she picks at it with the corner of her apron.

“Did you see Martin?” she asks.

“I saw the writing,” he says, and points down the lane.

From where you stand the bakehouse shutter is no more than a dark square. Close to, you can make out the pale slip against the wood. It is pinned high, where the grain has split along an old nail hole. The lower edge moves a little in the draught from the lane. There is not light enough on it to read a line from beside Alice.

She goes as far as the door. In the morning the latch had fallen of its own weight behind Martin. Now Alice puts one finger under its loose end, just where she must put it every morning with the key in her other hand. She leaves it resting there. From the seam at the jamb comes the smell of the day’s bread, cooler now and a little sour at the edge.

“I covered the tray,” she says. “It’ll have risen against the cloth by now.”

She looks down at her apron, at the corner she used to clean the key. “When it sticks, you wet the cloth. Don’t pull it off dry. Half the dough comes with it.”

The ledger has warmed where it lies against your ribs. At the guild table Martin had turned it so the entries faced you, the place for his name at the foot of the reckoning. On the facing leaf there was room enough for the few words he could attest without the sums. Out here the two leaves lie pressed together. The flour in the crease will still be there when they are parted.

Alice takes her finger from the latch. “He said he could put down that we brought it.” She nods at the book. “That’s something he saw.”

The paper stirs against the shutter again. In the morning the crier’s voice had reached all the way to the scales. From here there is only the dry little tick of the paper against the wood, and the boy waiting by the wall with nothing left in his hands.

Alice brings the key up level with the lock without putting it in. Then she looks from the slip to the ledger under your arm.

“Before I turn this,” she says, “will you read me what’s on the shutter?”

### Version B

At the mouth of the lane the dusk bell is still sounding, and the boy has to wait for the last stroke to tell Alice what he has put into her hand. It is her own key. She turns it by the bow until the bit lies along the crease of her palm.

“Martin ordered your oven sealed,” the boy says.

“Did you see him at the oven?” Alice asks.

“No. I saw the writing on the shutter.” He looks down the lane to be sure she knows which shutter he means, though there is only the one he could mean. “I can show you where it is.”

You walk the rest of the way together. The channel down the middle of the lane is wet, and the boy keeps to the strip beneath the walls where the stones are dry enough not to turn underfoot. At the bakehouse he stops short of the shutter and points. A pale corner lifts from the wood and settles again. From where you stand there are no words to be made out.

Alice puts her free hand on the sill. She runs two fingers along it and rubs the grit from them with her thumb. “That tray will be up to the cloth by now,” she says. “I tucked it close this morning. Should have given it another finger’s breadth.” She takes her hand off the sill. “It can come out of the trough again. It can’t go back to flour.”

The ledger is thickest at the spine, where it presses against your ribs. Martin had made room for it beside the seal. You had brought it to his table before dusk; he had said as much with his finger beside the delivery column. The place he meant to sign was at the foot of the reckoning, not beside the words that would say when the book was brought. There had been a clean page for those.

Alice brings the key as far as the height of the lock, then lowers it to her apron without trying it. “If I have to borrow an oven, the wood goes with the dough,” she says. “Then there’s the carrying. I can pay for some of it in bread, if there’s bread to pay with.” She folds the corner of her apron over the key and rubs where the teeth have left a mark in her palm. “I won’t give anybody your name for the rest.”

The boy lets his pointing hand fall. “It’s low enough to read,” he says. “I didn’t have to stand on anything.”

Alice shifts to the doorpost, leaving the shutter clear. In the failing light you can still see the pale corner the boy pointed out. One step would bring you close enough to try the writing. Behind you the dry strip along the walls is clear all the way to the mouth of the lane.

## Delayed callback

### Version A

“What of my promise?” you ask Alice. The carter has the cord drawn tight across the six sacks. While he waits, the end he has left for the knot trails in the wet between two stones.

Alice lifts the cloth off the small loaf. “You kept your word when Martin pressed you. Walk with me to the landing again tomorrow.”

She has brought the loaf down whole. The fold of cloth underneath has taken up a little damp, and she turns it to bring a dry part under the crust. At the bakehouse Martin had asked which way the flour was going. He had waited at the latch for an answer until Alice came up behind him with the peel. She had needed him to move before she could set the bread down.

“Mind that end,” she tells the carter. “You’ll have it in the river.”

He picks it up and lays it over his wrist. The cord slackens. Where it has pressed into the tarpaulin, a groove stays for a moment over the soft sack.

“That one will go down farther when I lift it,” he says. “The others keep their shape.”

“It weighed what I told you.”

“I’m not disputing the weight. I need to know which way I’m lifting it.”

Alice tucks a corner of the cloth back from the loaf. The crust has split at one side, not enough to open it but enough that you can see a little of the pale bread within. She puts her thumb there as if to break it and then takes her thumb away.

“I can bake this much again tomorrow,” she says. “These would take a while.”

The carter gathers in the slack by degrees. The tied mouth Alice pulled free is still uppermost. A little flour works out of a stitch as the cloth tightens over it. He moves the cord off the seam and tries the pull from there. This time nothing comes through.

Alice steps up onto the dry stone, leaving the carter room to get behind the sacks.

The cloth under the loaf has begun to slip off Alice’s wrist. She catches it against her hip and folds it back. “Come while I’ve still got flour on my hands,” she says. “Once the bread’s out, there’s the selling.”

Below the landing a little wash reaches the line of leaves and lifts two of them clear. One catches against the stone again. She has asked you for tomorrow; he is still waiting to be told where to take the flour now.

### Version B

She lifts the cloth off the small loaf. “You kept your word when Martin pressed you. Walk with me to the landing again tomorrow.”

A little of the crust has come away with the cloth. She picks it out of the weave and puts it in her mouth. The loaf is small enough to sit on her palm with room for her thumb against the heel. She holds it there while the carter shifts his grip on the cord.

“Didn’t tell him where, did you?” she says. “I heard him ask.”

“He can have the weights,” she says. “I don’t begrudge him those.”

The carter lets one end of the cord down onto the cloth and works his fingers open. In the slack, the fold he made over the stitching begins to lift. Alice sets the loaf back in its wrapper, holds the cloth down for him, and waits while he takes the end up again. He pulls only enough to keep the fold in place.

“Not so hard you lose the mouth of the sack,” she says.

He looks at the tied end she drew free for him. “That much I can keep in sight.”

A thin wash comes up the lowest step and runs off it. One of the leaves stays behind, flattened against the stone, and another goes back with the water. The carter moves his boot a little higher. There is room if he stands with his heel against the nearest sack. He keeps the cord in hand, his question of where to take them no nearer an answer.

Alice folds the cloth over the loaf again, but the corner won’t stay tucked. She turns the loaf over to pin it. “When he comes for the sums, put my name where I weighed. Not down with his.”

That much would fit. At the bakehouse the ruling came close to the last figure, but there was room to begin her name beside it without a stroke touching either. Martin had space below to write as large as he liked. His place was still empty when you left it, and the two lines above it had to be filled by dusk.

Alice rubs a thread of the wrapping between finger and thumb until it lies flat. “Tomorrow,” she says again. “Same way we came today. Mind that bottom step.”

Above the landing the lane is still light, though down here the wet on the steps is getting hard to tell from the stone. The carter waits by the six sacks. Alice has left you time to answer her.

## Meaningful consequence

### Version A

“Let me help you start again,” you say.

She slips the key into her pocket. “Come at first light. You can carry the wood. I’ll see about an oven.”

She says it as she told the carter to leave the mouth of the sack where he could get at it: one thing for him to do, and a reason plain enough not to need telling twice. The wheels go on a little longer. When they fall quiet, you can hear the lower edge of the notice touching the shutter. The nail through the wax holds it fast at the top, but the corner the boy had spoken of has worked loose enough to move.

Alice brings the loaf out from under her arm. The cloth is drawn so tight over it that the split shows as a little ridge. She puts the loaf back on the ledge and works a fold loose with two fingers.

“No sense wrapping it tight now,” she says. “It’s cold through.”

Behind the shutter the oven is cooling too. At this hour the bricks would still give back heat long after the last bread came out. From where you stand there is none to feel. The key makes a small sound in Alice’s pocket when she leans against the shutter to get at the cloth where it has caught underneath the loaf. She straightens and pulls the cloth free. She does not take the key out again.

The names are still in the book. The notice says no ledger was produced. It is a narrow sentence for what it leaves open. There is room on the paper for the weight of the missing stock, if whoever wrote it had meant to put a weight there. There is room for a name. By now you can see little but the pale sheet and the darker place where the seal is. You can still set the lines in order without reading them.

“Did he say how much he wanted?” Alice asks.

For a moment there is only Martin to put to the question, with his hand at the latch and Alice behind him with the peel. But she has turned her head towards the mouth of the lane. What the carter could answer for was six sacks, dry under the cover. There is no figure on the shutter to tell her what six will satisfy.

Alice rubs an edge of the cloth between her fingers. “The soft one won’t stand on its own,” she says. “He’ll have to lay it down.” She gathers the cloth loosely this time and takes the loaf off the ledge. A little of the crust is left there, no bigger than the head of the nail.

### Version B

“First light, then,” Alice says. “Here. Don’t go looking for me down at the landing.”
